# A Castle and No Dinner

The next day the children were being kept indoors as punishment.

Only Robert was allowed outside for a short time.

He knew exactly where he was going.

The gravel pit.

He found the Psammead sunning itself.

Robert had meant to make a careful, useful wish.

But when the moment came, every sensible idea disappeared from his head.

Then he thought about the games they played.

“I wish our house was a great castle,” he said.

The Psammead gave him a long look.

“Of course you do.”

<!-- block:a-real-castle -->
By the time Robert returned, the ordinary house was gone.

In its place stood a vast stone castle with towers, battlements, gates and echoing halls.

The other children were delighted.

They explored staircases.

They looked from the walls.

They imagined themselves kings, queens and defenders of a mighty fortress.

For perhaps twenty minutes, the wish was perfect.

Then they became hungry.

<!-- block:no-dinner -->
Martha and the other adults inside still behaved as though the ordinary house was there.

The magic did not seem to affect them in the same way.

Martha carried on with her work somewhere the children could not properly reach.

Dinner belonged to the ordinary house.

The children belonged to the castle.

They could see magnificent tables but no food.

They searched kitchens.

Empty.

Store rooms.

Empty.

Cupboards.

Empty.

“A castle,” said Cyril, “is much less impressive when it has no sandwiches.”

Outside the walls, something else was happening.

Strange figures had appeared in the distance.

Men in old-fashioned armour.

More followed.

They carried banners.

Robert stared.

A castle, apparently, had come with exactly the sort of trouble castles in stories usually had.

The approaching army began to surround the walls.

Their imaginary game was becoming a siege.
