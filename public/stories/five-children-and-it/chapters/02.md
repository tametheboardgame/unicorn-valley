# Golden Guineas

The next morning the children returned to the gravel pit.

They had learned from the beauty disaster.

This time, they told themselves, they would wish for something useful.

“Money,” said Cyril.

Everyone agreed.

Money could buy food, toys, bicycles and almost anything else worth having.

They found the Psammead half asleep in the sand.

“We wish for heaps and heaps of money.”

The sand-fairy sighed.

“How original.”

It swelled.

The sand shifted.

And suddenly gold coins spilled everywhere.

<!-- block:a-mountain-of-money -->
The children cheered.

They filled pockets, hats and handkerchiefs with golden guineas.

“We're rich!”

They marched towards the village.

That was when the trouble began.

The coins were real, but they were old-fashioned.

Shopkeepers stared at them.

A baker thought something suspicious was happening.

Other adults wanted explanations.

The children discovered that owning a fortune was not much use if nobody believed you had obtained it honestly.

They could not simply stroll into a shop carrying ancient gold and expect everything to be normal.

<!-- block:money-that-will-not-spend -->
They tried buying small things.

They tried explaining.

They tried looking trustworthy.

The harder they tried, the more suspicious everyone became.

By afternoon, being endlessly rich felt rather like being endlessly inconvenienced.

They escaped with very little to show for their fortune.

At sunset the guineas vanished.

The children checked their pockets.

Empty.

Robert looked disappointed.

Anthea looked thoughtful.

“We wished for money,” she said. “We didn't wish for any way to use it.”

Cyril groaned.

That was the second rule.

A wish did not care what you meant.

It only cared what you said.
